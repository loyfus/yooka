The best AI tools for small business are the ones that remove a specific, measured annoyance: the assistant that drafts customer replies, the automation platform that posts invoices, the document reader that extracts line items from a PDF. There is no single winner, and the most reliable setup is three to five tools, not twenty, chosen by workflow rather than by brand.

## How to choose without buying a stack you will not use

Most wasted AI spend comes from three mistakes: paying for two tools that do the same job, buying per seat for people who will barely open it, and starting with the most capable product instead of the one that finishes the task.

Filter every candidate through four questions:

- **What job does it replace?** Name the task in a sentence: "summarize supplier invoices into a spreadsheet" or "draft follow-up emails after a demo." If you cannot name the job, skip it.
- **Where does the data live?** A tool that reads your Google Drive is a different decision from one that reads SharePoint, and both are different from a tool that reads nothing at all. Integrations decide value more than model quality does.
- **How many hours a month does it return?** Multiply minutes saved per task by tasks per month, then by the loaded hourly cost of the person doing it. That number is what you compare against the price.
- **What happens when it is wrong?** In drafting, a bad output costs a minute. In bookkeeping, it costs a correction cycle and a strained client relationship. Ask who reviews the output.

## The right tool for each job

**Writing, editing and analysis.** One assistant is enough for most teams. Add a second only when the work splits cleanly: one for drafting and one for long documents. Running three at once creates inconsistent voice and a support burden.

**Research.** A general assistant will summarize what it finds, but it will not always cite the source. A research tool that returns links is worth its price when the output feeds a decision, a client deliverable or a compliance check.

**Automation.** Choose based on who maintains it. Visual builders are maintained by operations staff. Code-based platforms are maintained by a developer and let you self-host. The [automations that pay for themselves](/guides/automations-that-pay-for-themselves/) guide ranks the workflows worth building first.

**Documents and records.** Any tool that reads, classifies and writes back to a system is worth more than a tool that only writes text. Invoice capture, for example, belongs to a workflow rather than to an assistant, and [how to automate invoice processing](/guides/how-to-automate-invoice-processing/) covers that build in detail.

**Audio, image and video.** Separate products with separate billing, and the marginal cost per minute or per credit is where the budget goes. Generate short first, then decide whether long-form is worth it.

**Industry-specific software.** Accounting, scheduling, e-commerce and legal work each have a category of tools that know the domain rules. Those usually pay for themselves faster than a general assistant does.

| Media generation, if needed | US$5 | US$50 | US$150 |

| Ongoing maintenance | 1 hour a month | 5 hours a month | 12 hours a month |
| Monthly total | US$60 to US$65 | US$360 to US$460 | US$1,150 to US$1,550 |

The pattern worth noticing is that software cost scales with the number of people, but setup and maintenance do not scale in the same way. A fifteen-person team pays roughly twice the software bill of a five-person team, but the hours of setup and ongoing work are close to three times higher. That is the practical argument for keeping the number of tools small: every additional tool adds a setup and a maintenance obligation that does not get cheaper as the team grows.

The second pattern is that media generation, the line most buyers ask about first, is usually the smallest line in the budget. It is also the easiest to cut without hurting the workflow, which is a useful thing to know before signing an annual contract for a platform that produces assets nobody uses.

## How to roll out AI tools in 30 days

1. **Week one: measure.** Write down the five recurring tasks that eat the most hours across the team. Ignore anything under two hours a week; the subscription will not beat it.
2. **Week one: price your time.** Use the [calculators](/calculators/) to turn an hourly rate into an annual cost per person. That number is the budget line you are trying to beat.
3. **Week two: run a two-week trial on two candidates.** Set the same task in both, with the same inputs, and measure quality against the same checklist. Do not let a free month of enthusiasm decide it.
4. **Week three: buy one seat.** One person, one tool, one task, for thirty days. Resist the team-wide rollout; adoption without a use case is where the money disappears.
5. **Week three: write the rules.** A one-page note on what the tool may be used for, what needs review before sending, and what data may not be pasted into it. This prevents most incidents without a policy meeting.
6. **Week four: review the numbers and cancel what did not work.** Compare hours saved against total cost, including setup time. Keep one winner, cancel the rest, and note what the losing tool was actually better at.

## Three setups that work

**A solo consultant** working from a home office needs one assistant and one research tool. Their setup is roughly US$40 a month for drafting proposals, summarizing research and cleaning meeting notes. What they skip is automation: with no team, the hours saved have nowhere to go.

**An e-commerce operation** with 12 staff running a Shopify store needs an assistant for support drafting, an automation platform for order and review flows, and a document tool for supplier invoices. Their budget lands around US$120 a month, and the automation platform earns its place because it runs overnight while the team sleeps.

**A marketing agency** with 20 people needs one assistant per team, not per company, plus a workflow tool that connects briefs, feedback and reporting. Their monthly spend is closer to US$400, but the shared knowledge base inside one assistant replaces an expensive internal wiki. ## Common mistakes

- **Buying per seat for the whole company.** Seat costs multiply fast. Two or three heavy users beat twenty light ones.
- **Subscribing to overlapping products.** A general assistant, a writing tool and a document tool often duplicate each other. Audit what you have before adding.
- **Ignoring data terms.** Check what your assistant does with uploaded files and whether you can exclude your data from training. The [tools directory](/tools/) notes where this matters.
- **No review rule.** Unsreviewed AI output in customer-facing channels is the fastest way to lose a client.
- **Chasing capabilities instead of tasks.** Feature comparisons are a poor predictor of results. Compare completion of your actual tasks.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many AI tools does a small business actually need?** Three to five is the working range: one assistant, one automation platform, one document or data tool, and possibly one specialist for your industry. Every additional subscription should have a named task attached to it.

**What is the cheapest useful AI setup for a small business?** About US$20 a month for one assistant plus a similar amount for one automation platform covers most small teams. Spending more than that usually means paying twice for the same job, which the [comparison hub](/comparisons/) helps you spot.

**Are free AI tools good enough for a small business?** For drafting, summarizing and one-off research, often yes. Free tiers cap usage, file handling, history and integrations, so they stop working the moment a task needs to run on a schedule or touch customer data.

**Should a small business buy AI tools per seat or per workspace?** Per seat for the two or three people who will live in the tool, per workspace when the company shares one knowledge base. For agent-based workflows, the [guide on building an AI agent](/guides/how-to-build-an-ai-agent/) explains why shared tools and individual seats serve different purposes.
