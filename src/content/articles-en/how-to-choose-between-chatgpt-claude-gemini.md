To choose between ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, ignore the benchmark scores and test the three of them against the work your team actually does for two weeks. The practical default is Gemini if your business runs on Google Workspace, Claude if your work is long documents and careful writing, and ChatGPT for general use with the broadest feature set.

## What actually differs between them

The three assistants share a shape: a chat interface, file uploads, web search, memory and an API. The differences that matter in a small business are narrower than the marketing suggests and more consistent than people expect.

| Dimension | ChatGPT | Claude | Gemini |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strongest area | Broad general use, multimodal, app ecosystem | Long documents, careful writing, code | Workspace integration, very large context, search |
| Context window | Large, varies by plan | 200K tokens on paid plans | Very large, often above 1M tokens |
| File handling | PDF, spreadsheet, image, document | PDF, document, spreadsheet, code files | Native across Google Docs, Drive, Gmail, Sheets |
| Web research | Deep Research with sources | Not native; uses tools and connectors | Google Search and Deep Research |
| Image and video generation | Yes, including voice and video | No native image generation | Yes, image, video and audio |
| Writing style | Capable, more conventional | Most consistent on long formal writing | Varies more between prompts |
| App and extension ecosystem | Largest catalog of custom GPTs and apps | Smaller, but strong on connectors | Deep Google ecosystem integration |
| Code and developer tools | Strong, including code environments | Very strong, with terminal and IDE agents | Adequate, improving |
| Best fit | Whole-team default | Legal, finance, technical writing | Teams already on Google Workspace |

Two things are true across all three and worth stating plainly. Output quality between them is narrower than the vendor claims: for drafting a routine email, the difference is close to nil. And for anything consequential, the difference between the top model and the previous generation of the same model is smaller than the difference between a good prompt and a bad one.

The business tiers matter for a different reason than price: admin controls, shared workspaces, audit logs and data-processing terms. If your team will handle client or customer data, those terms decide more than the quality of the writing does.

## How to run a two-week test

1. **Collect ten real tasks before you start.** Pull actual work: the client email you rewrote last month, the contract you needed summarized, the research question you had to answer twice. A test built on made-up prompts tells you nothing.
2. **Write the evaluation criteria in advance.** Usually three things: accuracy on the specifics you care about, the amount of editing needed before use, and how often the answer required you to check a fact. Write them down before the first answer so you are not grading on vibes.
3. **Give each assistant identical instructions.** Same prompt, same attached file, same context. If you write a better prompt for one of them, you are measuring your own prompt-writing, not the assistant.
4. **Test the hard case, not the easy one.** Everyone wins on "write a product description". Test the task where the answer is long, the source material is messy, and getting it wrong would cost something.
5. **Measure editing time with a timer.** Open the output and count the minutes until it is sendable. Editing time is the metric that predicts which assistant will survive contact with your team.
6. **Check the file and format handling on your actual files.** Upload a 40-page PDF and a spreadsheet with merged cells. Upload the messy scan. Whatever the vendor's page claims, verify on your own document.
7. **Test the integration path, not just the chat.** If your work lives in Google Drive, test Gemini against Drive. If it lives in SharePoint or a connected system, check what each one can read natively and what needs a connector.
8. **Decide on a rule before you start.** The rule that works for most small businesses: use the one that wins on your five most frequent tasks, buy it for the people who need it, and review after 90 days with real usage data.

## How the choice plays out in practice

**A solo consultant** who bills by the hour rarely needs more than one subscription. They choose on document handling, because reading a 200-page report and pulling five specific findings is the work. That test has favored Claude in most side-by-side comparisons of long-document work, though the gap narrows on shorter files.

**A law firm** needs careful writing, dependable long-context reading and confidentiality. Claude handles long contracts without losing the thread, and the team tier provides the admin controls that a regulated environment expects. The decision here is driven by document handling and data terms rather than by price.

**A construction company** running on Google Workspace for email, drive and spreadsheets gets the most from Gemini, because the assistant reads the material where the work happens. A field supervisor who forwards a photo of a delivery note and asks for a summary does not need to copy anything into a chat window.

**A marketing team** with mixed needs often keeps two. A general assistant for daily drafting and a more capable one for long-form pieces. The second seat is usually cheaper than the third subscription that would otherwise appear in a trial.

## Common mistakes in the comparison

- **Comparing on benchmarks.** Public benchmarks do not reflect your documents, your tone or your approval process.
- **Testing only short prompts.** The assistants differ most on long, messy, multi-document work, which is exactly what tests tend to skip.
- **Buying a business tier for a two-person team.** Admin controls are worth paying for when several people need shared settings and audit trails. With two people and no data policy, an individual plan plus a written rule is usually enough.
- **Forgetting the API.** If you intend to build an agent rather than chat, model API pricing and limits may matter more than the subscription. Compare those separately.
- **Switching every quarter.** Model quality changes fast, but the switching cost is training and prompts. Settle, use it for two quarters, then review.
- **Ignoring where your data lives.** The assistant that reads your existing tools without an export step will be adopted; the one that requires copying and pasting will be abandoned.

## What to measure

| Metric | How to measure | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Editing time per output | Minutes from first open to sendable | Down 30 percent over 60 days |
| Tasks completed per week | Count of real work items closed with assistance | Rising steadily |
| Factual correction rate | Outputs needing a fact fixed before sending | Under 5 percent |
| Adoption | Seats used weekly | Above 70 percent |
| Cost per user per month | Total spend divided by active users | Under US$60 |
| Task coverage | Share of work the assistant can now handle unaided | Rising each month |

The metric that settles the question is editing time. Whichever assistant produces the least editing per week becomes the default for everyone else, because the time saved compounds across the team.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which is better, ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini?** It depends on the work. Gemini for teams already on Google Workspace, Claude for long documents and careful formal writing, ChatGPT for general use and the widest feature set. Many small businesses pay for two and assign one to each type of work.

**Can I subscribe to more than one of them?** Yes, and for many small businesses that is the right answer. One assistant for everyday work plus a more capable one for long documents usually costs less than a team bundle and covers more cases.

**Do these AI assistants handle my accounting or CRM data?** Not without checking. The consumer subscriptions have different data terms from the business tiers, and confidentiality obligations in your own contracts come first. Use a tier with explicit data-processing terms, and do not paste client records into a personal plan.

**How much should a small business spend on an AI assistant?** Between US$20 and US$60 per person per month for individual seats, and more for a business tier with admin controls, audit logs and contractual data terms.
